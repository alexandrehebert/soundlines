# New jack swing

Graph ID `genre:new-jack-swing`

**Review status** · Reviewed: 1987 emergence supported; early-1990s prominence not treated as end

**Reviewed** · 2026-11-04

## Sources checked

- [Carnegie Hall: History of New Jack Swing](https://timeline.carnegiehall.org/genres/new-jack-swing) — Museum history links Teddy Riley’s production to 1987–88 recordings and describes the blend of R&B, hip-hop rhythms and gospel vocals.
- [Nate Sloan and Charlie Harding, How Hip-Hop Became Hit Pop](https://www.luminosoa.org/books/177/files/55b07a27-6050-411c-ac31-85a843243e51.pdf) — University of California Press open-access book discusses new jack swing as a hybrid filling airwaves from 1987 to 1992, without claiming the style vanished afterward.
- [Oxford University Press, The Musical Quarterly: “This Year’s Model”](https://academic.oup.com/mq/article/105/3-4/320/6780154) — Peer-reviewed article treats new jack swing as a recognizable production framework and brand by the late 1980s.

## Assessment

Kept circa-1987 emergence supported by “I Want Her” and the rise of Teddy Riley’s production. Removed 1995 endpoint and unsupported peak phase; the early-1990s end of chart dominance is not the end of the style.

## Remaining uncertainty

The name is associated with a 1987 Village Voice profile, while musical precursors and early recordings predate widespread naming.
