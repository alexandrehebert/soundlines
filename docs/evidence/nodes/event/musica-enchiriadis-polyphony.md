# Early polyphony is notated in a surviving manuscript

**Review status** · reviewed

**Reviewed** · 2026-11-04

## Sources checked

- [Corpus Christi College, Parker Library, MS 260](https://iiif.biblissima.fr/collections/manifest/a2835eb34f24ad2e5853560acaa1f33fb44090c6) — Manuscript catalog dates this copy to the late tenth century and describes Musica enchiriadis as one of the earliest witnesses to polyphony and dasian notation.
- [Cambridge University, Earliest known piece of polyphonic music discovered](https://www.cam.ac.uk/research/news/earliest-known-piece-of-polyphonic-music-discovered) — Research report describes a practical polyphonic addition dated to the early tenth century, while emphasizing that the exact emergence of polyphony is unknown.
- [Cambridge Core, Reading and singing: genesis of occidental music-writing](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/early-music-history/article/abs/reading-and-singing-on-the-genesis-of-occidental-musicwriting/6E0E68ADF62F6D2822A4AEC2314787F8) — Scholarly study dates the oldest complete Musica enchiriadis witness to the late ninth century and distinguishes text chronology from extant copies.

## Assessment

The current circa 900 date is a broad marker, but the note should distinguish the late-ninth-century treatise, surviving manuscript witnesses (some later), and an early-tenth-century notated addition. It documents some of the earliest written evidence for polyphony, not the beginning of polyphonic practice.
