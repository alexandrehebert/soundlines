## Sources checked

- [C. Bechstein: 1930–1945 history](https://www.bechstein.com/en/die-welt-von-bechstein/tradition/1930-1945/) — Manufacturer dates the Neo-Bechstein’s presentation to 1931 and says it was innovative but commercially unsuccessful, then a niche product.
- [Kunstmuseum Den Haag: Neo-Bechstein electronic grand piano](https://www.kunstmuseum.nl/en/collection/neo-bechstein-electronic-grand-piano) — Museum record dates its Neo-Bechstein specimen to 1932, corroborating the early-1930s launch while distinguishing object date from model debut.
- [Powerhouse Collection: Wurlitzer model 200A electric piano](https://collection.powerhouse.com.au/object/611665) — Museum history dates Wurlitzer’s first electric piano to 1954; notes 200/200A models appeared in 1968/1974 and were high-volume products.
- [Smithsonian Music: Fender Rhodes Electric Piano](https://music.si.edu/object-day/fender-rhodes-electric-piano) — Smithsonian search extract says Rhodes became popular in the 1970s, especially in rock, R&B, and jazz; the page itself returned an internal help-desk error.
- [Oxford Academic: Electronic and Computer Music—The Background to 1945](https://academic.oup.com/book/27042/chapter-abstract/196323611) — Search excerpt describes the Neo-Bechstein Piano (1931) as a modified acoustic piano using pickups and electronic amplification; direct page returned a cache miss.
- [Electric piano and Rhodes piano (Wikipedia, original graph citations)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_piano) — Checked Wurlitzer struck-reed mechanism and Rhodes dates against museum records and Smithsonian history; this is a lead, not the evidentiary basis.
- [Rhodes piano (Wikipedia, original graph citation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhodes_piano) — Dates the full-size Fender Rhodes to 1965; cross-checked against Smithsonian and museum histories.

## Assessment

The previous 1954 start confused Wurlitzer’s first model with the broader electric-piano category. Bechstein and a Dutch museum establish the earlier Neo-Bechstein (1931 debut; 1932 specimen); Powerhouse confirms Wurlitzer in 1954; Smithsonian describes Rhodes popularity in the 1970s. Model chronology is now explicit.

## Remaining uncertainty

“Electric piano” spans different electroacoustic designs; the exact boundary between early electroacoustic pianos and later electric-piano models depends on classification. Smithsonian Rhodes evidence was available through search indexing, not a readable page.
